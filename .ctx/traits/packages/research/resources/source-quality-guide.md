# Source Quality Guide

The canonical grade definitions are in research-standards.md. This guide provides examples and a decision method; it does not redefine the scale.

## Rating Method

1. Identify the source type and whether it is primary, secondary, or commentary.
2. Check author or institution authority for the exact subject.
3. Inspect method, sample, data provenance, conflicts, and peer or regulatory review.
4. Check recency relative to the claim and whether a newer source supersedes it.
5. Rate the source for this claim, not by publisher reputation alone.
6. Record why the grade applies and what corroboration is still needed.

## A-Rated Examples

- Systematic reviews or meta-analyses of strong studies.
- Large, well-designed randomized controlled trials.
- Primary regulatory decisions, official standards, statutes, final rules, and court opinions.
- Top peer-reviewed technical or scientific work with transparent methods and data.
- Authoritative public datasets with documented collection methods.

Use A-rated evidence for critical quantitative, medical, technical, legal, and safety claims whenever available.

## B-Rated Examples

- Clinical practice guidelines from established professional bodies.
- Cohort and case-control studies with reasonable methods.
- Government, central-bank, intergovernmental, or regulator reports that summarize primary evidence.
- Technical white papers from established organizations with disclosed methods.
- Reputable expert analyses and industry research with transparent sources and conflicts.

Clinical guidelines remain B-rated by default because they synthesize evidence and recommendations; rate the underlying primary evidence independently.

## C-Rated Examples

- Reputable journalism with named sources and editorial review.
- Substantive company engineering posts about the company's own systems.
- Analyst reports, conference talks, textbooks, and expert commentary.
- Secondary summaries used for context or discovery.

Do not use C-rated material as the sole support for a critical claim when primary evidence is available.

## D-Rated Examples

- Unreviewed preprints, preliminary benchmarks, conference abstracts, and early trial reports.
- Opinion posts, promotional white papers, unsourced market claims, and stale material.
- Sources with weak methods, undisclosed conflicts, or unverifiable data.

Use only as a lead or to describe an emerging claim, with strong caveats and independent corroboration.

## E-Rated Examples

- Anonymous or fabricated material.
- Deceptive, plagiarized, or materially manipulated sources.
- Unverifiable social posts, testimonials, spam, and claims that misrepresent cited work.
- Sources with no usable provenance for the assertion being made.

Do not use E-rated sources as evidence. They may be mentioned only when the research question itself concerns misinformation or public narratives.

## Domain Notes

### Medical And Health

Prefer systematic reviews, strong trials, primary regulatory records, and transparent surveillance data. Separate evidence of efficacy from guidance or recommendation. Record population, sample, endpoint, follow-up, conflicts, and applicability.

### Technology And Engineering

Prefer standards, peer-reviewed work, source code or primary documentation, and reproducible benchmarks. Vendor documentation is primary for documented product behavior but not neutral evidence for comparative claims.

### Business And Finance

Prefer filings, audited statements, regulator data, official statistics, and transparent research methods. Treat market forecasts and vendor-sponsored surveys as method-dependent rather than authoritative by default.

### Social Science And Policy

Prefer registered studies, transparent datasets, official statistics, and replicated work. Check sampling, construct validity, geography, dates, and causal overreach.

### Legal And Regulatory

Prefer primary law: statutes, regulations, final rules, official guidance, and court opinions. Secondary commentary helps interpretation but does not replace current primary authority.

## Disagreement And Composite Use

- Rate each source independently; do not average several weak sources into one strong source.
- Confidence in a claim depends on the strongest relevant evidence plus independent corroboration.
- When qualified reviewers disagree, record both rationales and use the lower grade until the conflict is resolved.
- A source may receive different grades for different claims if its authority or method differs by topic.
